# Commercial

Boring Things is an AI that helps people track and manage life administration across appliances, memberships, subscriptions, utilities, and service providers, by finding manuals, answering questions, scheduling repairs and maintenance, offering upgrades, and more.

This document describes the commercial opportunity of Boring Things, including competitors, market size, revenue potential, pricing, costs and risks.


## Competitor landscape

### Direct competitors

`Overlap` below is a directional estimate based on public feature sets, not measured user overlap.

- `Homer` (`~75-85% overlap`)
  - Strongest current match to the appliance-first version of Boring Things.
  - Public feature set includes home inventory, manuals, receipts, maintenance reminders, AI chat over manuals, expenses, collaboration, and contractor management.
  - Signals of age, size, and traction: founded in 2017; LinkedIn lists `2-10 employees`; App Store shows `4.3/5` from `137` US ratings; Apple has featured it; Homer markets itself as the successor to Centriq.
  - Boundary: strong on home ownership and appliances, weak on broader household admin such as memberships, subscription optimisation, and open-banking insights.

- `HomeZada` (`~60-70% overlap`)
  - Mature digital home-management platform spanning inventory, maintenance, projects, documents, contacts, expenses, and home finance.
  - Signals: founded in 2011; LinkedIn lists `2-10 employees`; consumer premium is priced at `$99/year`; App Store shows `2.8/5` from `42` ratings with version history back to 2011.
  - Boundary: broader home-asset framing than day-of-need assistant. It looks more like a home-ownership system than an everyday household copilot.

- `HomeBinder` (`~40-55% overlap`)
  - Covers centralized home records, maintenance, inventory, and utility or home-service setup through `HomeBinder Assistant`.
  - Signals: founded in 2012; LinkedIn lists `11-50 employees`; partner-distributed rather than direct-to-consumer self-serve.
  - Boundary: less consumer AI and less self-serve. Stronger as a real-estate or move-in leave-behind than as a daily assistant.

- `Hartley` (`~55-70% overlap`)
  - Close to the `household admin copilot` framing: document organisation, renewals and deadlines, AI question answering, tailored advice, and local services.
  - Signals: UK company incorporated on `23 May 2025`; App Store shows `3.6/5` from `8` GB ratings and a fast release cadence through April 2026.
  - Boundary: very early, small, and not yet proven. It is one of the few competitors that extends beyond appliances into broader home admin.

- `HomeNog` (`~55-65% overlap`)
  - Very close to the appliance-memory wedge: auto-captures appliance labels, finds manuals, tracks warranties and maintenance, answers questions, and checks recalls.
  - Signals: official site claims `thousands` of items organised and promotes a `v2.0` AI engine.
  - Implication: this is a focused version of one of the strongest early Boring Things use cases.

- `Homebly` (`~45-60% overlap`)
  - Digital record for appliances, systems, warranties, service records, and shared household access.
  - Signals: app and site launched recently; App Store version history begins in March 2026 and currently shows `1` rating.
  - Implication: another sign of a fragmented but active long tail.

- `Maizon` (`~45-60% overlap`)
  - Home manuals, warranties, maintenance calendar, expenses, contractors, and a home-value snapshot.
  - Signals: app launched at the end of 2025; App Store shows `5.0/5` from `2` ratings and paid plans.
  - Implication: overlap is real, but traction still looks minimal.

### Adjacent competitors and substitutes

- `General-purpose workspace / notes` (`Notion`, `Evernote`)
  - Strong substitute for storage, tagging, and reminders if the user is disciplined enough to build the system manually.
  - Weak for extraction, enrichment, automated discovery, and service actions.
  - Realisable share of the Boring Things value proposition: `~20-35%`, higher for power users and much lower for stressed or disorganised users.
  - Why it matters: Notion has `100M+` users worldwide and `79K` App Store ratings, so the substitute is common even if it is not purpose-built.

- `Password manager / secure vault` (`1Password`)
  - Good substitute for `where did I put that number, login, or membership ID?`
  - Weak for physical assets, appliance context, maintenance, utilities, and service orchestration.
  - Realisable value: `~10-20%`.

- `Money management and insights` (`Snoop`, `Emma`)
  - Strong substitute for subscription discovery, bill tracking, budgeting, and some switching prompts.
  - Weak for appliance manuals, home-service history, warranties, and grounded answers about owned physical things.
  - Realisable value: `~25-40%` overall, but `~60-80%` of the financial-oversight slice.
  - Signals: Snoop has `500K+` Google Play downloads and `6.8K` App Store ratings; Emma was founded in 2017, has `11-50 employees`, and `1.3K` App Store ratings.

- `Services marketplaces` (`Checkatrade`, `Rated People`, `MyBuilder`)
  - Strong substitute only at the `find and hire someone` moment.
  - They do not remember the home for you; they become useful after the problem is already defined.
  - Realisable value: `~10-25%` overall, but `~70-90%` of the repair-booking slice.
  - Signals: Checkatrade has `615` employees on LinkedIn; Rated People says `10,000+` tradespeople and `3M+` homeowners; MyBuilder says it has `276,317` tradespeople, `11,878,124` jobs, and `2,766,722` reviews.

- `Comparison providers for financial products` (`MoneySuperMarket`, `Compare the Market`, `Confused.com`)
  - Strong substitute for savings capture around insurance, utilities, broadband, and some maintenance services.
  - Weak for memory, inventory, documents, and recommendations grounded in what the household actually owns.
  - Realisable value: `~10-20%` overall, but `~60-80%` of the switching slice.
  - Signals: MoneySuperMarket says `13M+` people save money with it; Compare the Market says it provides `millions of quotes` each year across `675+` providers and `1,284` products; Confused.com says it has been helping consumers for more than two decades and employs `250+` staff in Cardiff.

- `Asset tracking / inventory` (`itemit`, `HouseBook`, home inventory apps`)
  - Good substitute for cataloguing and proof-of-ownership.
  - Weak for household reasoning, admin workflows, and action-taking.
  - Realisable value: `~20-35%` overall.
  - Signals: itemit says it is trusted by `3,000+ teams worldwide`; HouseBook has `4.3/5` from `43` App Store ratings.

- `Calendar / task tools`
  - Good substitute for recurring maintenance and renewal reminders.
  - Weak for capture, memory, reasoning, and service-provider context.
  - Realisable value: `~10-15%`.

- `Email / cloud drive / photo library`
  - Most common free substitute today. Users can search old emails, PDFs, and photos when needed.
  - Weak because recall depends on user naming, filing, and patience under stress.
  - Realisable value: `~15-25%` for organised users, much lower in emergencies.

### Legacy / failed / defunct

- `Centriq`: platform shutdown announced on its site, with users redirected to Homer.
- `Encircle Home Inventory` (free consumer product): support ended in late 2025.
- Lesson: the category is real, but `record keeping alone` is probably not enough. The products that survive appear to add either a strong action loop, a partner channel, or a sharper AI/assistant layer.

### Fit to use cases

| Use case theme | Direct home-management apps | Finance apps | Marketplaces / comparison | Generic tools | Gap Boring Things can exploit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Truth-on-demand` | Strong for appliances and home docs; weak for memberships and non-home admin | Weak except for bills and subscriptions | Weak | Possible, but manual and brittle | Cross-domain answers spanning appliances, memberships, subscriptions, utilities, and service records |
| `Problem solving` | Medium to strong where AI/manual retrieval exists | Weak | Weak until the user already knows what service to buy | Weak to medium for organised users | Grounded answers that use owned context, not just generic web knowledge |
| `Preparedness` | Strong on maintenance and reminders | Medium on bill due dates and spending alerts | Weak | Medium if the user maintains calendars and lists | Low-input upkeep driven by extraction, enrichment, and proactive nudges |
| `Optimisation` | Medium on home costs and replacement timing | Strong on recurring spend and bill analysis | Strong on switching once prompted | Weak | Tie savings and replacement guidance to what the user actually owns |
| `Action / handoff` | Medium where contractor features exist | Medium where switching flows exist | Strong once the need is defined | Weak | Diagnose first, then package the right context for booking or switching |

### Takeaways

- The market is fragmented. There is no single dominant product that spans appliance memory, broader household admin, financial oversight, and action-taking.
- The strongest direct threat today is `Homer` for appliance-first workflows and `Hartley` for broader household-admin positioning.
- The strongest substitute threat is not a home-OS startup. It is the combination of `Notion + finance app + marketplace + email search`, which can cover a surprising amount of the job for motivated users.
- The best early wedge still looks like: `photo/document capture of appliances and household paperwork`, `grounded answers in moments of stress`, `light financial/admin intelligence`, and `handoff into repair or switching when needed`.
- That wedge keeps direct home-management apps boxed into `home-only` territory while making generic substitutes feel manual and fragmented.


## Revenue model

- Commission on repair and maintenance bookings.
- Consumer subscription for premium features.
- Referral/lead fees for vetted service providers.
- Marketplace booking fee where applicable.
- Affiliate revenue for consumables and replacement parts.
- White-label product for partners (insurers, property managers, utilities).


## Market sizing framework

### UK entry market

- Primary wedge: appliance ownership + maintenance/repair workflows.
- Secondary wedge: recurring household spend oversight.
- Initial paying segment: carers and households with multiple appliances and outsourced maintenance.

### Global expansion market

- Expand to regions with mature open-banking APIs and high service-marketplace adoption.
- Prioritize countries with similar household admin pain profile and high smartphone usage.

### Data points for market sizing

- UK had `29.0 million households` in 2025 (ONS).
- UK consumers spent `£733m` in 2024 on repair of household appliances (ONS series).
- 26% of UK adults accidentally entered a subscription in the prior year (Citizens Advice/Opinium).



## Operating cost model

### Core cost buckets

- Cloud hosting/runtime.
- Postgres and storage.
- LLM inference and OCR/document extraction.
- Open Banking provider fees.
- Engineering and product.
- Compliance/legal and insurance.
- Customer support and service operations.

### Cost-control principles

- Start with free tiers and usage caps.
- Keep AI calls retrieval-grounded and token-efficient.
- Defer expensive workflows (notifications automation, booking ops) until unit economics are clear.


## Commercial risks and mitigations

- `Low willingness to pay early`: bundle clear savings outcomes and trust features.
- `High support overhead`: constrain MVP scope and automate intake/triage.
- `Regulatory and liability exposure`: stage risky features behind explicit terms and controls.
- `Platform dependence on providers`: design provider abstraction for Open Banking and AI.
