# Marketing

Boring Things is an AI that helps people track and manage life administration across appliances, memberships, subscriptions, utilities, and service providers, by finding manuals, answering questions, scheduling repairs and maintenance, offering upgrades, and more.

This document describes the marketing strategy for Boring Things, including branding, messaging, expansion strategy, campaign ideas and metrics.

## Marketing goals

- Prove that `problem solving` messaging drives attention and first-use intent.
- Prove that `truth-on-demand` builds trust and activation after a user tries the product.
- Establish `optimisation` as the next monetisable story after the initial utility has been demonstrated.
- Build a story that is specific enough to feel urgent, while leaving room to expand into broader household admin over time.

## Positioning and brand

### Core narrative

Boring Things helps people solve annoying household problems fast by knowing the boring details they never want to look up.

### Promise hierarchy

- Lead promise: helps you figure things out.
- Proof layer: already knows or can find the right boring details.
- Expansion layer: helps you feel more in control over time.

### Possible straplines

- `Figures out the boring stuff.`
  - Best fit with the current `problem solving` wedge. Short, active, and easy to pair with practical demos.
- `Knows the details. Solves the problem.`
  - Stronger at expressing the link between `truth-on-demand` and `problem solving`.
- `Your household, figured out.`
  - Broader and more brand-like, but less sharp for direct-response acquisition.
- `Answers for the stuff you own.`
  - Good for product explanation, especially when leading with appliances and household items.
- `Finds the details that fix the problem.`
  - Slightly longer, but strong if the product needs a more explicit explanatory line early on.

Lead with `Figures out the boring stuff.` for brand and campaign use, and keep `Knows the details. Solves the problem.` as a supporting line for landing pages and app-store style descriptions.

### Personality

- Practical rather than aspirational.
- Calm under pressure rather than chirpy or overly friendly.
- Capable without sounding technical.
- Brief, useful, and grounded in specifics.
- Slightly wry about modern household admin, but never sarcastic about the user.

The brand should feel like:

- the helpful person who knows where the manual is
- the calm friend who can tell you what to do next
- the organised household memory you wish you already had

The brand should avoid:

- sounding like a generic AI productivity assistant
- exaggerated claims about changing your life
- overly cute or whimsical language that weakens trust
- cold enterprise or fintech language

### Colour scheme

Text: Black
Shading: Use a fixed set of defined greys for sections, cards, box backgrounds and the app background

Primary: #713CDD (Purple), for mainstream calls to action, borders, dividers, progress bars, icons
Secondary: #AF8FEF (Lilac)
Highlight: #1DC956 (Grass), for 'special content' (asides, marketing promos etc)
Highlight 2: #ECBF93 (Beige)

Semantic colours: #DD3C3C (danger/error), #F49D25 (warning), #3C8CDD (info)

### Message architecture

- `Busy independents` are the first serious marketing audience.
- `Problem solving` is the headline because it is legible, urgent, and easy to demonstrate.
- `Truth-on-demand` is the support layer that makes the problem-solving promise believable.
- `Optimisation` is the next expansion story once users trust the product and have stored enough information for deeper insights.
- `Preparedness` is best treated as a retention and lifecycle message rather than the initial hook.
- `Families` and `carers` should appear through examples and proof of range, but not as co-equal launch audiences.

## Key messages

### Problem solving

Primary acquisition message. Lead with the moment where something is broken, confusing, or blocking progress.

- "Knows what that error code on your washing machine means."
- "Can tell you how to set the time on your microwave."
- "Finds the right light bulb for that broken lamp."
- "Pulls out the right manual, then gives the short answer."
- "Helps you figure out what to do next when something in the house stops working."

### Truth-on-demand

Support message. This is the capability layer that makes the primary story credible.

- "Finds your boiler model before the engineer asks."
- "Knows if your membership is still active."
- "Pulls up the warranty, receipt, or service record without a hunt through emails and drawers."
- "Can tell you which provider, account number, or renewal date is attached to a thing."
- "Remembers every service, receipt, and repair so you don't have to."

### Optimisation

Secondary message for later lifecycle, especially relevant to families and more established households.

- "Spots utility bill spikes before they become a problem."
- "Shows how much is going out each month across subscriptions, memberships, and household services."
- "Helps identify which recurring costs are worth cancelling, switching, or renegotiating."
- "Turns a scattered household paper trail into a clearer picture of where the money goes."

### Preparedness

Later retention message focused on staying ahead of preventable hassles.

- "Keeps every renewal date in one place."
- "Reminds you about the boring maintenance that prevents bigger problems later."
- "Keeps a running history of what was fixed, replaced, or serviced and when."
- "Makes future problems easier because the details are already there."

## Channel strategy (UK-first)

- Prioritize search-driven acquisition around concrete problem queries such as `what does this washing machine error code mean`, `how do I set the time on my microwave`, and `what light bulb does this lamp need`.
- Build content and landing pages that show a specific household problem being resolved, rather than describing the product as a general household-organising app.
- Use paid social and short-form video only when the creative demonstrates a tangible before-and-after problem-solving moment.
- Treat `truth-on-demand` as the support layer in acquisition creative: show that Boring Things finds the right manual, receipt, or record and uses that to answer quickly.
- De-emphasize broad `get your life organised` positioning in early acquisition because it is less legible and less urgent.
- As the product expands, test `optimisation` messaging with families and higher-admin households, but keep it secondary to the initial problem-solving wedge.

## Early campaign ideas

- `What does this error code mean?`
  Search and content campaign built around confusing appliance and device error messages, with a demo showing Boring Things finding the source information and turning it into a short answer.
- `How do I set this?`
  Short-form creative and landing pages focused on fiddly household setup tasks like clocks, timers, remotes, and settings that people routinely forget.
- `What part do I need?`
  Campaign focused on replacement parts and consumables such as light bulbs, filters, batteries, and other small but irritating household fixes.
- `Finds the manual, then gives the answer`
  Secondary trust-building campaign that explains why the product can answer well: it stores the boring details and cites the right source instead of guessing.

## Success metrics

- Acquisition: click-through rate, signup intent, and cost per qualified visit on `problem solving` hooks.
- Activation: percentage of users who add a first thing or document and receive a first useful answer.
- Trust: percentage of answers where users view, expand, or rely on cited source material.
- Engagement: repeat questions per user and weekly active usage after the first successful answer.
- Expansion: share of activated users who return for a second category of value such as `optimisation` or `preparedness`.
- Commercial: conversion to paid and referral or partner conversion once downstream offers exist.
